Overview

Indigenous peoples experience
before, during, and after WWI

Before the First World War, legislation across Australia stripped Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people of their political voice, freedom of movement, and basic rights.

Despite all of this, over one thousand Indigenous Australians still decided to enlist, many hiding their identities just to get in. In the trenches, they actually experienced equality. But when they came home, any form of recognition or reward was never kept.

This assessment looks at Indigenous people's voices and experiences before, during, and after the war, and how WWI was a catalyst for political activism.[10]

Chapter I Before the War

To what extent did the pre-1914 state protection acts and Defence Act 1903 silence the political voices and rights of Indigenous people before WWI?

The Defence Act 1903 provided the legal structure for Australia's new defence forces.[9] When Australia was proclaimed an independent nation in 1901, professional soldiers and volunteers from the individual British colonies were in South Africa fighting in the Boer War. These colonial forces were transferred to the Commonwealth on 1st of March 1901. The Defence Act 1903 provided a legal structure for the new Australian Defence Force to work under. It is still in force today.

In the first decade after federation, the ADF was very small. It was made up of full-time paid members (the smallest group), part-time paid members (the militia), and part-time volunteers. Its focus was on local defence. Only volunteers could serve outside of Australia until the act was changed after WW2.

However, by 1911 the Northern Territory and every state except Tasmania had a protection act, giving the chief protector or protection board extensive power to control Indigenous people.[3] In some states and in the Northern Territory, the chief protector was made the legal guardian of all Aboriginal children, displacing the rights of parents.

"Everything that related to a concentration camp was there in [Cherbourg]. You could not move without getting a permit. Cherbourg is built right on Bramble Creek. About 100 metres or so away from the river was the farmer's house. We had to get a permit to go down and fish there. We had to get a permit to go to Murgon, which was four miles away six kilometres and it had a time set on it. If you came back five to ten minutes after that time expired, you would be put in jail for, maybe, a weekend. And they brought the curfew in. All lights had to be out at nine o'clock. If you were found out after dark or after the lights had gone out, you were put in jail. They even put searchlights on the vehicles the police, the superintendent and chased black fellas everywhere, hither and thither, throughout the night hours."
Eyewitness testimony, Cherbourg Aboriginal Settlement. Source: Parliament of Australia, Senate Stolen Wages Inquiry, 2004[7]

In response to the outbreak of the First World War, Australian Parliament passed the War Precautions Act 1914.[5] This act gave the Governor-General the power to make regulations for "Securing the public safety and defence of the community." The 1914 War Precautions Act was made to protect Australia during the war; it essentially gave the government powers it hadn't gotten before, very powerful powers.

The War Precautions Act 1914 was used to make more than 100 regulations, including:

  • Censoring what could be reported in the press
  • Restricting the sale of certain products
  • Limiting public gatherings including sporting events
  • Restrictions on speech and communication
  • Forcing residents and citizens of enemy origin to move to internment camps
  • Fixing the price of basic food items
  • Limits on interest rates

They claimed that these and other extraordinary regulations were required to protect Australia and its citizens during the time of war. Additionally the 1914 War Precautions Act gave the executive government for example, the Governor-General, Prime Minister and other ministers the power to make laws instead of Parliament. Using the defence powers in Section 51 "Legislative powers of the Parliament" of the Australian Constitution,[8] the government could make regulations which would've otherwise been unlawful at the time. It also gave the government the power to control the political activities of Australians.

However, the act was later repealed in 1920, just 6 years after the act was created.[6]

Key Legislation

Defence Act 1903
Established the legal framework for the Australian Defence Force. Barred Indigenous Australians from enlisting on racial grounds.

State Protection Acts (by 1911)
Every jurisdiction except Tasmania enacted legislation granting "chief protectors" sweeping legal authority over Indigenous people including guardianship of their children.

War Precautions Act 1914
Granted the executive extraordinary wartime powers over 100 regulations controlling speech, movement, and political activity.

Historical Significance

These acts didn't work by themselves. All the acts combined built a connected system of legal options: the Defence Act kept Indigenous people out of military service, the protection acts stripped away their civil freedoms, and the War Precautions Act handed the government control over nearly every part of civilian life. Put together, they made it almost impossible for Indigenous people to have any political voice at all.

Primary Source

The Cherbourg testimony is a rare piece of primary evidence illustrating the daily reality of life under the protection act system restrictions on movement, curfews, jailing for minor infractions, and constant surveillance.

Source: Parliament of Australia, Senate Stolen Wages Inquiry (2004).[7]

Chapter II Enlistment & Identity

How did the restrictions against people not from majority European descent force Indigenous people to alter their identities to enlist, and what does this say about how their voices were viewed from wartime authorities at the time?

The Enlistment Rule

The AIF recruiting handbook was explicit:
"Aborigines and half-castes are not to be enlisted. This restriction is to be interpreted as applying to all coloured men."

This rule was only relaxed in May 1917 via Military Order 200(2), which allowed men with "one parent of European origin" to enlist.[1]

Perspectives

During the war, authorities treated Indigenous identity as a liability in peacetime but useful when they needed soldiers. Their voices were only really acknowledged when their bodies were wanted.

The restrictions against Indigenous people or people from not majority European descent forced many to conceal their real origins in order to serve their country.

Some claimed to be Indian, Māori, or of full European descent. Not only did they conceal their real origins, but some also anglicised their names.[1] However, some recruiters just dismissed their real origins and allowed them to enlist regardless.

The reason that Indigenous people weren't allowed to enlist was because they were deemed a security risk or "undesirable" in peacetime, yet their identities were commodified for the warfront. Most likely the reason for this being the "reason" is because they didn't conform to the nationalism that was being pushed across Australia. During their service, Indigenous people experienced equality and equal pay whilst serving and in uniform however these rights were stripped from them upon returning.

Whilst other veterans received settlement land grants and access to specific clubs for veterans (RSLs), Indigenous veterans were excluded or denied these rights despite actually serving. This says that when they're needed, they were given basic human rights; however, as soon as they weren't needed, they were just thrown in the trash essentially.

Even though a lot of Indigenous people wanted to serve their country so much so that they would alter their identities just to enlist they still weren't given basic human rights after the war.

By 1917, fewer enlistments and more reinforcements required at the front led to recruiters watering down the "Majority European Descent" rule. Recruiters were allowed to accept men who could satisfy a medical officer they had "one parent of European origin".[1] However this was only after two attempted votes for conscription were defeated, once in October 1916, and the other in December 1917.

Overall, throughout the entire wartime period, the authorities proved that they only acknowledged Indigenous people and other people not of majority European descent when they needed them because they had no other choice and even then they made it really hard for them to enlist. This proves they really had no voice and were disregarded.[4]

Chapter III In the Trenches

To what extent did the condition of the trenches allow the voices and status of Indigenous ANZACs to be heard and valued as equals compared to their normal life?

The extreme conditions of the trenches allowed Indigenous people to be heard and valued as equals for all the enlisted soldiers were in the same conditions.

They all had equal pay and had to eat the same rations, and suffered the same perils. They earned respect among the other soldiers because in those conditions, their comrades were not looking at race, but were instead grateful that these men had wanted to help. [1]

"No discrimination on the battlefield, and certainly none in the military hospitals."
Queensland nurse, 1919. Source: Department of Veterans' Affairs[1]

Which was a brief glimpse into what could've or should've been the same regardless of the war. They should've been treated as equals. Due to the fact that they were all in the same conditions and the same place, Indigenous people in the war were treated equally and actually respected. Hence, their voices were heard more.

The equality Indigenous soldiers experienced in the trenches was only ever temporary. Back home, nothing had changed which made it all the more obvious just how much they were being denied in their everyday lives.

Equality of Service

In the trenches and military hospitals of the Western Front, the ANZAC tradition of mateship created a levelling effect. For many Indigenous soldiers, for many it was probably the first time they had been treated as equals.

Some non-Indigenous veterans formed close bonds with their Indigenous comrades, though those friendships usually didn't last once they returned to a segregated Australia.[1]

Historical Terms

ANZAC Australian and New Zealand Army Corps

AIF Australian Imperial Force; the volunteer military force raised during WWI

Home Front civilian life and conditions within Australia during the war

Western Front the major theatre of WWI operations in France and Belgium

Chapter IV Post-War Exclusion

How did the exclusion of Indigenous veterans from the Soldier Settlement Schemes and pension benefits demonstrate that the Australian Government refused to hear or reward the efforts of Indigenous people that served in the war?

The Soldier Settlement Scheme

The Soldier Settlement Scheme was a post-war program designed to reward returning veterans with land grants dividing large rural estates into farming blocks. Indigenous veterans were excluded entirely.[4]

In some cases, Aboriginal land was divided under this scheme and then granted to non-Indigenous soldiers compounding the injustice with a direct act of further dispossession.

What Was Denied

Land grants Soldier Settlement Scheme access refused

Pension benefits repatriation support withheld

RSL membership access to Returned Services League clubs denied in many states

Public recognition contributions largely erased from official records

Indigenous veterans being excluded from the Soldier Settlement Scheme and pension benefits showed that the Australian Government only cared when they actually required Indigenous people to enlist in the war.

After the war, when they were no longer needed, they were just back to how they were treated before despite serving in the war and making a big impact. This shows that the government never actually cared about Indigenous people, and only allowed more Indigenous people to enlist due to mass casualties and declining enlistments, because they needed to in order to win.[4]

The treatment they received after the war was pretty terrible. They went back to a segregated Australia and were denied land grants, pension benefits, and so many benefits that other non-Indigenous veterans received despite Indigenous people having served in the same terrible conditions and trenches as other veterans that weren't Indigenous. [1]

In some cases, Aboriginal land was actually taken and handed out to non-Indigenous veterans under the scheme so not only were Indigenous veterans denied the benefits, their own communities lost even more land on top of it.[4]

The difference was obvious and intentional: in the trenches, their service was recognised because they were needed. When they came home, that recognition was revoked. The government's choice not to give Indigenous veterans the same rights as non-Indigenous soldiers wasn't a mistake; it was a clear sign of whose voices Australia was actually willing to listen to.

Chapter V Political Awakening

How did the post-war disillusionment of returning Indigenous soldiers directly fuel the voices of organised political activism and civil rights groups in the 1920s and 1930s?

Indigenous soldiers returning to the whitewashed Australia and not receiving the same rewards as other veterans made veterans realise that Australia was mired in hypocrisy. This hightlights both change and continuity in Australian society; whilst discrimination against Indigenous Australians continued after the war, growing support for Indigenous rights signalled important social and political change.

They realised that if Indigenous veterans were willing to die for their country, despite being treated terribly, then they were entitled to equal rights and equal respect. Some veterans leveraged their military service proving they had loyalty and courage to challenge the hypocrisy of white Australia, demanding that because they fought for their country, they therefore deserved their citizenship rights.[3] Therefore, the veterans who stood up for Indigenous Veterans formed somewhat of an alliance.

Because these veterans who were extremely well respected realised this and started to voice their opinion, it directly fuelled political activism and civil rights groups.

Founded in 1924, the Australian Aboriginal Progressive Association (AAPA) is the country's first politically organised, united Aboriginal activist group.[3] Which was massive progression at the time, and turned out to be extremely useful and over time allowed Indigenous people to receive more and more rights.

"They realised that if Indigenous veterans were willing to die for their country, despite being treated terribly, then they were entitled to equal rights and equal respect."
Historical analysis of post-war Indigenous activism. Source: Australian Human Rights Commission (2023)[3]
The AAPA 1924

The Australian Aboriginal Progressive Association (AAPA), founded in 1924, stands as the country's first politically organised, united Aboriginal activist group born directly from the disillusionment of returned Indigenous servicemen and women.[3]

Its creation was a real turning point, moving from isolated voices speaking out to a coordinated political push. It laid the groundwork that future civil rights campaigns would build on.

Legacy & Continuity

The activism born from WWI's disillusionment did not end in the 1920s. It laid the groundwork for the 1938 Day of Mourning, the 1967 Referendum (in which 90.77% of Australians voted to count Indigenous people in the census), and decades of subsequent civil rights campaigns.

The voices that had been silenced for so long weren't going to stay quiet.[3]
